The constant= bit comes from

> 1/diff(qnorm(c(.25,.75))/2)
[1] 1.482602

so the logic is pretty much that mad() computes the SD of a normal distribution with the same IQR. This makes sense for an outlier-contaminated normal distribution, and not so much elsewhere. 

It is fairly easy to modify the constant for other symmetric distributions like the standardized t distribution, somewhat less so for asymmetric distributions. That is, provided you actually want the SD of the underlying distribution (sans outliers).

If you set mad=1, then you lose the connection with SD, which may not be much of a loss. However, the question that I see is whether it offers anything beyond the simple IQR.

 Thanks, that's a helpful perspective.

My use case is a little different from estimating the SD of an underlying distribution. I'm working with signed-error distributions that are often skewed, and my primary interest is a robust description of the observed error spread rather than a normal-consistent estimate of ?.

In that setting, the default 1.4826 factor feels like a model-based calibration rather than part of the dispersion measure itself. Using mad(x, constant = 1) keeps the scale tied directly to the median absolute deviation observed in the data.

Regarding your IQR comment, that's a fair question. My intuition is that MAD and IQR emphasize somewhat different aspects of the distribution. IQR is determined entirely by the 25th and 75th percentiles, whereas MAD is based on the distribution of absolute deviations from the median. For diagnostic work, I've found the latter useful because it stays centered on the typical deviation from the target value.

So I see raw MAD less as a substitute for SD and more as a robust measure of "typical error magnitude."
- Thomas S

STRING_PTR_RO(x)[i] will not check bounds and will not check that the
element is not NULL and not a free node. STRING_PTR_RO only works on
standard vectors, and will not work on alternate vector representations.
Also, it will return (const SEXP *) (void *) 1 when the character vector is
of length 0, or may return a pointer to a random place in memory. In
general, I would suggest

const SEXP *y = NULL;
if (Rf_xlength(x)) y = STRING_PTR_RO(x);

or something similar in order to avoid anything weird involving 0 length
access.

I do not know of any other differences to note.

Iris Simmons gave an excellent explanation. The only thing left to note
is that some ALTREP classes currently have STRING_ELT methods that
return CHARSXPs not protected from garbage collection:

https://bugs.r-project.org/show_bug.cgi?id=19135
https://github.com/gagolews/stringi/issues/354
https://github.com/tidyverse/vroom/pull/638
https://github.com/apache/arrow/issues/51198

If your code can be expected to process strings from vroom or arrow and
you chose STRING_ELT (instead of STRING_PTR_RO which would materialise
the vector), you may need to add some workarounds.

Hello

I am a research assistant intern for biostatistics at the department of public health, Aarhus University (AU). I have been tasked with understanding why normalized residuals between R and Stata are different when using mixed models (i.e. the nlme package in R).

What I have found is that the residuals are both theoretically computed the same way: normalized_residuals = sigma^(-1) * Lambda^(-1/2) * residuals. This is based on the works of Jos? C. Pinheiro and Douglas M. Bates. The nlme package in R references their book "Mixed effects models in S and S-PLUS" (2000), while Stata references their article "Computational Methods for Multilevel Modelling" (1998).

However, the way the matrix square root (of Lambda) is computed is different between the two programs, the result of which can have a rather significant impact on whether or not the normalized residuals actually seem to follow a normal distribution. According to my superiors, this has been an issue for the department in past projects.

The way R computes the matrix square root is through the Cholesky decomposition A = LL^T (using the "chol" function from FORTRAN77). I cannot access the source code for Stata, but it seems like Stata uses Cholesky decomposition as well, however using a Cholesky decomposition algorithm starting at "the bottom right" of the matrix instead of "the top left" such that A = L^T L. Using these two methods for Cholesky decomposition, I have been able to recreate the normalised residuals from R and Stata respectively (on some datasets that I cannot share due to GDPR rules). When using QQ-plots on the normalized residuals calculated with R and the ones calculated with Stata, the two plots imply different conclusions about the normality of the residuals.

My question is that, in principle, could one not use several different methods for computing a matrix square root and possibly come to significantly different conclusions? For example, why is the Cholesky decomposition used over the eigenvalue decomposition (which seems like the most intuitive way to compute the matrix square root to me)?

To me it seems like all methods for computing the matrix square root are theoretically equally good in terms of normalization (removing heteroscedasticity and correlation) and work equally well on large sample sizes, but they can differ quite significantly on smaller sample sizes.

I am interested in hearing whether there is a specific reason that one matrix square root is used over another and if you know whether there is an aspect of "small sample optimality" when choosing method. Thanks in advance!

Note, that I have also asked this question on cross-validated ("Normalized residuals in mixed models, R (nlme) vs Stata<https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/677243/normalized-residuals-in-mixed-models-r-nlme-vs-stata>"), where I have provided an example on the data 'RatWeight' from package 'heplots'.

   I've answered this on CrossValidated. The tl;dr version 
(copied/quoted) is:

 > The fundamental answer to this question is that the 'normalize' 
option to residuals is not intended to make the residuals closer to 
Gaussian (despite the name); it's intended to "whiten" the residuals, 
i.e. to remove heteroscedasticity (not taken into account in this case 
because this GLS model is modeling only correlation, not 
heteroscedasticity ...) and/or correlation.

  I also suggest there that (1) Cholesky is slightly more 
computationally efficient and (2) I'm not sure that the normality of 
normalized residuals is the right way to test for *multivariate* 
normality of the within-group residuals anyway ...

The root(!) cause here is that the idea of normalizing transforming to N(0, I) is simply not well thought through. I don?t even think that Doug Bates would disagree. Basically such normalizations are only unique up to orthogonal rotation and different rotations can give quite different results.

As you notice, Cholesky-based approaches depend on reordering data. As far as I can tell, you basically get normalized residuals from regressing variables on the set of previous variables,  This may make good sense if data are on a time series, but not really otherwise. Eigen-decomposition at least doesn?t have that problem, but it is still not easy to interpret what it means, and you still have issues like the fact that eigenvectors can be sign-flipped (and rotated in the case of multidimensional eigen spaces). 

Since R is open source, there is nothing keeping from checking whether you can reproduce the Stata results. I think you can get the reverse order Cholesky just by reversing rows/cols.

However, my hunch is that it would be better to come up with a technique that is targeted towards specific  departures from the model assumptions. If you are looking for outliers, One idea could be to try and predict each observation from all the others and do a QQ plot of the standardized residuals. This doesn?t make the residuals uncorrelated, but that?s not really important for QQ plots. You may be able to do this efficiently using something like rank-1 updates of the inverses involved, but even if not, computers have become a lot faster since year 2000? You might also try to exploit substructures in the matrix.

- Peter D
