Hello?everyone

I'm sharing a new open-access resource that you may find useful: The Institutional AI Readiness Pack <https://instats.org/publications/the-institutional-ai-readiness-pack> and its accompanying instruments catalogue <https://mzyphur.github.io/ai-readiness/instruments.html>.?

Universities increasingly have AI policies, but many still lack an evidence-based picture of how AI is actually used and governed across their research environments. Without that picture, they cannot reliably identify gaps across policy, people, systems, and process, nor can they determine how to strengthen the institutional capabilities that enable rigorous and responsible AI use in research. That is the gap this pack closes.?

The pack accompanies Responsible AI in Academic Research: A Competency Framework for Research Training <https://instats.org/publications/responsible-ai-in-academic-research>, which defines five dimensions of institutional readiness and the capabilities that underpin them. It turns that framework into an institutional self-assessment, surveys for graduate students and supervisors, gap-analysis and action-planning tools, model policy and disclosure materials, agreements, and briefings for university leaders. Every instrument is openly licensed under CC BY 4.0 and available in HTML, Word, and PDF.?

The framework is non-prescriptive about which maturity level an institution should occupy. Nobody outside your university is grading the exercise. There is no prize for a high score and no penalty for a low one. The real risk is relying on an inaccurate picture.?

For researchers, graduate students, and universities that want more structured training on AI in academic research, Instats is running a four-part seminar series <https://instats.org/course/agentic-ai-for-academic-research1> this month and next on how to understand and use AI for academic research. It covers AI as a Research Method, Responsible AI for Academic Research, Verifiable AI for Academic Research, and Building Agentic AI Research Teams. Each seminar stands alone, but together they provide a coherent progression for developing the knowledge and skills needed to use AI effectively for rigorous and honest research.

I hope you find the pack useful, and I hope you can join us for the seminar series. Please feel free to share both with students and colleagues working in research leadership, training, integrity, policy, or graduate education.


Best wishes

Dear All,

I?m taking advantage of this email to share a small reflection. It has been
a long time since I received an email from someone asking a question about
R or geostatistics, and I think this says something about how much LLMs
have taken over this kind of exchange.

These models were trained, among many other sources, on the knowledge that
people shared for years on Stack Overflow, forums, mailing lists, and
similar communities. Now, instead of asking another person and waiting for
an answer, we simply ask ChatGPT and usually get an answer immediately.

But I wonder how long this model can sustain itself. If we stop asking each
other questions and discussing possible solutions, where will the new
knowledge come from? We do not only need to reproduce what is already
known; we also need to invent new things. Are LLMs really able to generate
the kind of innovation that these communities produced over decades, or are
they mainly very good at recombining what humans have already created?

There is also a social aspect that I think we may be losing. Through these
discussions over the years, it was nice to get to know people, exchange
ideas and sometimes discover possibilities for collaboration. In that
sense, it was a little like going to a conference: of course you go to
learn, but you also go to meet people, network, discuss ideas and perhaps
start new projects.

It feels as though ChatGPT is gradually taking over many of those
interactions. It is incredibly useful, but I wonder what we might be losing
at the same time.

Best,

Andres Diaz

Dear Melanie, Andres and R-SIG-GEO members

Although I sympathise with your views, one has to consider that there are also relevant benefits when using LLMs when developing open-source software. One key aspect is improving the quality in OSS development. 

I lead a team of four developers of an R/Python package for satellite image time series (?sits?). The package has about 100,000 LOC (code + tests) and depends (directly or indirectly) on more than 130 other CRAN packages. Recently, we started using Claude Code to help us develop new functions, improve testing and find errors. The contribution of LLMs was significant in all dimensions. It?s the equivalent of adding one full-time experienced developer to our team. 

One should bear in mind that the vast majority of R packages are done by small teams. Many of those are university professors and/or researchers whose day job is not software development. If tools such as Claude Code help to improve the quality and reliability of OSS, there is a significant gain for the community. 

Best regards
Gilberto

Dear Gilberto, Melanie, Andres and R-SIG-GEO members:

I am going to say it bluntly - I am with Gilberto 100% on the use of LLM 
in facilitating package development and deployment, while I do resonate 
with Andres's concern that we need to develop from new knowledge and not 
let those LLMs take over.

My own experience is exactly that. I constantly ask Claude Code and 
Codex to develop the initial scripts for me, but will never let the 
scripts go live or to my students without me reading through them line 
by line (takes quite sometime, but much less than I thought it might). I 
am constantly amazed by how well those LLMs make brilliant choices that 
I myself or my team might not necessarily think of (as Gilberto put it 
brilliantly, we are just university professors/researchers and our day 
job is really not software development, actually, quite far away from 
that). But there are also times those LLMs will be extraordinarily 
verbose on the smallest things, which we could usually quickly spot and 
move out.

So, I think Andre's concerns are real and valid, but the usefulness of 
the LLMs is also real and valid, the middle ground is that we as 
experienced scholars should do what experienced scholars always do - 
examine whatever was produced by a program, be it an R package, or an 
LLM output, with rigorous, trained scrutiny and correct anything that 
needs correcting or at the very least put up questions when knowledge is 
lacking.

Just like when the search engines first appeared in the early 2000s.

My two cents.

Best,

Danlin

Dear Sebastiano 

It may help to distinguish two perspectives in this discussion. Your email addresses the impact of LLMs on academic progress and publication. The other perspective (arguably more germane to R-sig-Geo) is that of R package developers and users: what LLMs mean for the R package ecosystem and for CRAN. On that question, I would argue the effect is positive.

Consider the numbers. According to CRAN maintainers, there were 18,293 submissions to CRAN in the first half of 2026. These included 1,992 brand-new packages. Also, there were 1,273 archived packages.

Setting aside the roughly 2,000 submissions for new packages, some 16,000 remain. Since an update typically takes more than one submission, this suggests that between 10,000 and 15,000 distinct packages received at least one maintenance, patch, or feature update in that period. That is good news for R and for CRAN, and a measure of the community's continued effort.

The CRAN Task View on spatial data lists about 200 packages; a rough count suggests that some two-thirds have been updated since August 2025. This is a positive sign of community involvement. Whether their authors would benefit from using LLMs is, I think, a good question for this list.
